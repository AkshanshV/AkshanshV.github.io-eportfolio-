About me

Adapting is the whole job

I moved to Canada at one year old, which meant growing up caught between cultures in a fast paced environment. That taught me how to adapt early, and that same instinct now shapes how I work in security, where the landscape never holds still.

I chose York University for practical reasons. It offered a comfortable, close to home transition into higher education. What actually stuck was the Computer Security program at Lassonde, which rewarded hands on, dynamic problem solving over memorizing theory, which is exactly how I like to learn.

That approach came from my high school computer science teacher, Mr. Smith, who taught me to completely reframe failure. A broken piece of software isn't a permanent roadblock, it's simply a puzzle waiting for a targeted solution. It's still how I approach debugging a program, adjusting to a new city, or starting a new job: stay level headed and solve the puzzle in front of you.

Outside of coursework, traveling keeps that adaptability sharp, since new cultures and unfamiliar situations force you to figure things out on the fly, the same muscle I use navigating a constantly shifting digital landscape. Basketball and working out do the opposite job. They build the discipline and focus to stay steady when the environment does shift.

Goals and reflections

Where I've grown, and what's still in progress

These portfolios track my development against three undergraduate competencies I set out to grow this term. Rather than listing every self assessment I completed, each one below pairs a real workplace goal with a specific artifact and an honest reflection on what it actually taught me.

Competency: Critical Thinking and Problem Solving

Building a habit of evidence before action

Goal: improve my Tier 2 and Tier 3 troubleshooting process by documenting each step and testing at least two reasonable alternatives before escalating, especially when an Intune application package fails to deploy correctly.

In IT and cybersecurity you can't afford to guess. Jumping to conclusions on a Tenable vulnerability scan or a Tier 3 escalation can accidentally break systems for a large number of users, so this competency directly protects the people on the other end of every ticket.

Retell

During my rotation at Hydro One I was assigned a batch of security vulnerabilities. Instead of patching them one at a time, I looked for the repetitive pattern across tickets and built automated scripts to remediate the same issue across multiple endpoints at once.

Relate

This put the Systems Development Life Cycle into practice in a live environment, and it's a direct extension of my troubleshooting goal, documenting steps and testing alternatives before escalating, just applied at scale instead of ticket by ticket.

Reflect

Manual patching is tedious and error prone at enterprise scale, and I learned that the hard way. What I didn't expect was that deploying even a well tested script meant coordinating sign off across three separate departments. That taught me automation is only half the skill, the other half is earning the trust of the teams whose systems you're touching.

Competency: Agility and Adaptability

Learning the systems that run underneath everything

Goal: get fully comfortable with Microsoft Intune, Entra, and Tenable, and build the ability to research and remediate new vulnerabilities as they appear, rather than relying on someone else to have already solved it.

The tech field doesn't hold still. An app can deploy cleanly through Intune one week and break on half the fleet the next after an unexpected update. Getting comfortable with ambiguity is less a skill I can check off and more a habit I have to keep building.

Retell

My role rotated weekly between help desk support, depot hardware setup, application packaging, and vulnerability remediation. I was rarely doing the same type of task two days in a row.

Relate

University teaches these tools in isolation. The rotation forced me to see how they connect. Understanding how we package and push software through the Company Portal, for instance, gave me a much clearer picture of how we deploy security patches at scale.

Reflect

I started the placement unsure whether I could keep up with that much context switching. By the second month it stopped feeling chaotic and started feeling like the same skill applied in different rooms. The honest critique is that I still lean on documentation more than I'd like when a tool behaves unexpectedly, and that's the next thing I want to build real fluency in.

Competency: Effective Oral and Written Communication

Translating, not just fixing

Goal: write ticket notes end users can actually understand, avoiding heavy IT jargon in roughly 90% of closed tickets, while keeping backend notes precise enough for the teams I escalate to.

Knowing how to fix an issue isn't enough on its own. I have to be able to explain it to two very different audiences in the same afternoon, and that skill matters as much for my career as the technical work does.

Retell

On the help desk I was constantly switching between talking a frustrated, non technical user through a fix in plain language and escalating the same issue to a backend team using precise, jargon heavy terminology.

Relate

This mirrors the organizational culture concepts from my coursework. I was learning Hydro One's internal culture in real time by watching the language, structure, and approval processes each team defaulted to.

Reflect and critique

I got noticeably better at shifting register on the fly, but the rapid context switching meant I never got to write a long form technical guide from scratch, the kind of writing that actually tests whether you understand something deeply enough to teach it. That's a gap I'm going to close by proactively asking for a documentation project in my next placement, rather than waiting for one to land on my desk.
